I just finished Avatar: Seven Havens, and mehn, it's not easy, because the potential is huge and the execution kept frustrating me.

Don't get me wrong, there's a lot to like. The premise is interesting, the world-building opens up some cool possibilities, and the mystery behind the Cataclysm gives the story room to grow. But I kept feeling the show could have been much better than what we got.

Seven Havens is the new animated series from Avatar creators Michael Dante DiMartino and Bryan Konietzko, set 225 years after The Legend of Korra. Book One, titled Survival, has 13 half-hour episodes and streamed weekly on Paramount+ from October 9 to October 30, 2026.

Pavi is a young Earthbender living in Ellora, one of the seven Havens left after a world-shattering cataclysm. She learns she's the next Avatar after Korra, but in this world the Avatar is seen as the Destroyer, not the savior. Along with her long-lost twin, Nisha, she has to find out where they came from.

**A quick heads up: this review has full spoilers for Book One, including the finale.**

## What worked

![REVIEW: Avatar: Seven Havens (2026)](/images/posts/avatar-seven-havens-review/what-worked.jpg)

### A world that fears the Avatar

This is where the show asks its best question. At the summit with the other Havens, the envoys are attacked, Nisha steps in to protect them, and her identity as the Avatar comes out. The leaders turn on her instead of thanking her, and Kayoko even wants her dead. What happens when the person meant to keep balance isn't trusted by the people she's supposed to protect?

### The third act and the truth about their parents

The last stretch of the season is the strongest part, and it's the emotional heart of the show.

Through most of the season, Nisha believes their parents abandoned them, while Pavi insists there has to be a better explanation. In the finale, "The Root Chakra," the Anomaly pulls both sisters back into a shared childhood memory. Their parents were planning to leave with Pavi and leave Nisha behind, because her power scared them. Young Nisha caught them, and her fear of being abandoned set off the Anomaly. The burst of energy brought down a rockslide that apparently killed their parents and injured Pavi.

It's a heavy reveal because it changes Nisha completely. She isn't just reckless. She's someone who was rejected as a child and has been living with the consequences ever since. Pavi's reaction matters just as much. She doesn't only learn that Nisha was behind their parents' deaths. She learns that Nisha hid it from her. Her anger makes sense, even though it's a tragedy for both of them.

Then Pavi stops running from her identity, declares herself the Avatar, and destroys the Anomaly. The sisters part ways, with Pavi heading off to find her next lesson and Nisha staying in Ellora.

I wish the show had given the fallout more room. A reveal this big should change the relationship between the twins, and I wanted to sit in that for longer before the story moved on. Still, it gives them a really interesting conflict to carry into Book Two.

## What didn't work

![REVIEW: Avatar: Seven Havens (2026)](/images/posts/avatar-seven-havens-review/what-did-not.jpg)

### Good ideas, frustrating choices

My biggest problem is how the people in charge behave. Fear is the show's main theme, and the Anomaly, a mysterious force that feeds on fear, is how that theme messes with people's judgement. I understand that. But there's a difference between characters whose thinking is clouded and characters who just make needlessly irrational decisions.

Kayoko is the best example. Ellora is dealing with the Anomaly and the Faceless, and her answer is to use the Anomaly's power to trap the Faceless and the Avatar. That only makes things more dangerous.

When a character makes a terrible choice, I want to see why. Show me the inner conflict and what pushed them there, even if I disagree. Without that, the decision feels like a convenient way to move the plot, and I was more annoyed at the characters than invested in what happened to them. The concept is fine. The writing doesn't always turn it into believable behavior.

### The animation: why does the world feel so lifeless?

This is my biggest technical complaint. I'm not a fan of the new visual style, but I'm fine with a new series not looking like The Last Airbender or Korra. My issue is the backgrounds. They often feel frozen, like the characters are walking through a painting.

You notice it most when the world should be reacting. Crowds in a panic, storms, political standoffs, whole communities under threat. People should run, crowds should feel alive, and the surroundings should show how big the danger is. Instead, some scenes feel strangely empty. Simple animation can work, but this world needs to move.

### The tone and the humor

Avatar has always been for younger viewers too, and I'm not asking for Seven Havens to get darker or more violent. But a lot of us watched Aang and Korra growing up and are now in our late teens or twenties. The show sometimes leans so hard into a kid-friendly tone that it forgets that audience.

The random phrases and jokes are occasionally funny, but too often they feel out of place. Aang's story had plenty of silly moments, and they worked because they suited the characters and the scene. Here, when the show is dealing with childhood trauma, lost history and persecution, the lighter moments sometimes undercut it instead of balancing it.

---

## Mixed feelings

### The chakra system

I liked that the show brings back the chakras, which Aang worked through with Guru Pathik in The Last Airbender. Pavi unlocks her Root Chakra in the finale, which fits since it's tied to fear and survival, the season's main theme.

My worry is that it turns into a checklist. Emotional growth isn't that neat, and you can beat one fear while still struggling with another. If each chakra is a stage in Pavi's growth, I hope each one gets real depth and isn't just a way to unlock new powers.

### The White Lotus

The White Lotus has always been about protecting knowledge and supporting the Avatar. So how does it work in a world where whole communities see the Avatar as a threat, and where the leaders of the Havens have their own interests to protect? The show barely touches it. It's a great question, and I hope Book Two goes there.

---

### Final Verdict

**My Rating: 6.2**

A world that fears its own Avatar and a civilization that has lost its history are great foundations, and the twins give the show a real emotional core. If you're an Avatar fan, it's worth watching, especially for the finale. I'm cautiously optimistic about Book Two, and I just hope the show trusts its own ideas more.
