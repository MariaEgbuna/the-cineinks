The Legend of Vox Machina is one of the best things animation has given us this decade, and I'm putting it in the Hall of Fame while the show is still running. This show has already given me enough to know exactly where it stands.

Here's my confession though. I went in expecting a fun D&D adaptation, something silly to put on in the background. What I got instead was a story with characters I actually care about, animation that keeps getting more ambitious with every season, and a plot that somehow manages to top itself every single time it comes back. Shows usually peak and coast. This one just keeps climbing.

* * *

### The Plot (In a Nutshell)

Vox Machina is a band of misfits, mercenaries, and full time bar tab dodgers who stumble into becoming the people who actually save the world. What starts as a group taking odd jobs for coin turns into a fight against a vengeful undead lord, a coalition of ancient chromatic dragons burning entire cities to the ground, and now, in the current season, something even older stirring in the dark. It's set in Exandria, the fantasy world built by Matthew Mercer, and every arc raises the stakes without losing the characters in the noise.

The premise works because of how unglamorous the group's starting point is. These aren't chosen ones or prophecy driven heroes. They're broke adventurers who take a mercenary job because they need the coin, and the story lets the stakes grow organically from there instead of announcing on day one that these seven people are destined to save the world. That grounded starting point is part of why the eventual scale of what they take on hits as hard as it does.

### My Take

What actually gets me about this show is how it never treats "funny" and "devastating" as opposites. One episode has Grog getting into a drinking contest, and two episodes later someone's dead and the whole group is quietly falling apart from grief. That whiplash should feel jarring. Instead it feels like how people actually process loss, one minute laughing to survive it, the next minute completely gutted.

And the thing that keeps me coming back season after season is that nothing here feels like filler. Season one sets up the group and the Briarwood revenge arc. Season two throws the Chroma Conclave at them, an entire coalition of dragons burning Emon to the ground and forcing the group to scatter and regroup from nothing. Season three brings that fight to its actual conclusion, closing out Thordak and the rest of the Conclave while still finding room to deepen what everyone's carrying emotionally after all that loss. Season four splits the group up to deal with the fallout on their own terms, which sounds like it could drag, but instead turns into some of the most character focused writing the show has done. Every season picks up exactly where the last one left the characters emotionally, not just where the plot left off.

The animation itself has quietly leveled up every single time too. Season one already looked sharp for a show funded off Kickstarter money, but by season three the fight choreography got noticeably more ambitious, camera work started doing things you'd expect from a much bigger budget, and the character designs picked up small details that reward people paying close attention. Most shows either lock in a visual style and stay there or slowly get lazier as the seasons pile up. This one keeps spending more effort exactly where you'd expect a show to start coasting.

### The Characters

**Vax'ildan:** The one who carries the weight nobody asked him to. Vax spends most of the show quietly deciding he's the one who has to make the sacrifice, the one who has to take the hit, and watching him wrestle with his own mortality while falling for someone he's terrified to lose is some of the most affecting material in the whole series.

![The Legend of Vox Machina Is Peak](/images/posts/the-legend-of-vox-machina-is-peak/image-3.jpg)

**Vex'ahlia:** Sharp, guarded, and constantly doing the math on what everything costs, literally and emotionally. Vex starts out as the twin who keeps score and slowly turns into the one holding the entire group's emotional accounting together.

![The Legend of Vox Machina Is Peak](/images/posts/the-legend-of-vox-machina-is-peak/image-4.jpg)

**Percival de Rolo:** Percy is what happens when revenge actually works. He gets his vengeance on the Briarwoods and it hollows him out instead of healing him. Watching him try to figure out who he is once the thing he's been building toward for years is actually done is some of the best character writing in the series.

![The Legend of Vox Machina Is Peak](/images/posts/the-legend-of-vox-machina-is-peak/image-5.jpg)

**Grog Strongjaw:** The muscle, the comic relief, and somehow also one of the most quietly moving arcs in the show. Grog spends seasons wrestling with whether being strong is the same thing as being good, and the show never treats his intelligence, or lack of it, as a punchline without also giving him real depth underneath it.

![The Legend of Vox Machina Is Peak](/images/posts/the-legend-of-vox-machina-is-peak/image-6.jpg)

**Pike Trickfoot:** The moral center of the group, and one of the only people in Vox Machina who actually seems to know who she is from episode one. Pike's faith gets tested constantly, and instead of that being a weakness, it becomes one of the show's steadiest emotional anchors. Even when the group scatters or falls apart around her, Pike is usually the one holding the thread that pulls everyone back together, not through force, but through the quiet certainty of who she's chosen to be.

**Scanlan Shorthalt:** The bard whose ego is only matched by how much he actually needs this group, even when he pretends otherwise. His arc around fatherhood and abandonment sneaks up on you after seasons of dick jokes and increasingly unhinged songs, and by the time it actually hits you realize how much groundwork was quietly laid under all the comedy.

**Keyleth:** The druid figuring out what leadership actually costs, torn between duty to her people and loyalty to the group that's become her real family. Her slow burn arc with Vax is handled with more patience than most live action shows manage.

### Why It Works

Part of the magic here is where this show actually came from. This wasn't a studio pitch. Critical Role, the tabletop group behind all of this, launched a Kickstarter in March 2019 asking for $750,000 to fund a single 22 minute animated special. Within an hour they'd cleared their goal. By the time the campaign closed 45 days later, fans had pledged over $11.3 million, making it one of the most funded film or television projects Kickstarter had ever seen. Amazon took notice, picked up the distribution rights before the season even finished production, and turned what was supposed to be one short episode into a full series.

That origin story matters because it shows up in the finished product. This is a show built by people who actually played these characters at a table for years before any of it hit a screen, voiced by the same cast who lived through every dice roll and improvised joke in the original campaign. You can feel that lived in quality in every performance. Nobody's reading lines cold, they're revisiting people they already know intimately. Laura Bailey isn't just voicing Vex, she's replaying years of choices she personally made as that character live on stream, and that history bleeds into every line delivery in a way you can't fake with a normal casting process.

Critically, the show has backed up the hype too. All three completed seasons hold a perfect 100 percent score on Rotten Tomatoes, with critics consistently pointing to the animation, the humor, the voice work, and the fight choreography as reasons it stands apart from most adult animation. The show keeps figuring out how to get better instead of just coasting on goodwill.

### Final Thoughts

I'm putting an unfinished show in the Hall of Fame, and I'm not apologizing for it. Some things don't need a finished story to prove they're great, they just need to keep giving you a reason to trust them every time they come back. Vox Machina has done that four seasons running, and I have zero doubt season five closes it out exactly the way this cast and crew have built it up to.

If you love fantasy with actual heart, characters who feel like people instead of archetypes, and animation that never stops getting more ambitious, this is for you. If you think D&D is just nerds rolling dice in a basement, this is also for you, because it'll change your mind by episode three.
