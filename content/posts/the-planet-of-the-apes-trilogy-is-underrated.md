This was a franchise that had already been rebooted once in 2001 under Tim Burton and landed with a thud critically despite turning a profit. The idea of going back to the well again felt desperate rather than promising. It was the start of one of the most quietly consistent trilogies in modern blockbuster filmmaking.

Three films. $1.5 billion combined. A franchise that got better with every single entry. Hall of Fame. No debate.

---

## The Premise

A genetically enhanced chimpanzee named Caesar grows up caught between the human world that raised him and the ape world he belongs to, eventually leading a rebellion that changes the course of both species forever. It starts small, a San Francisco scientist testing an Alzheimer's drug on chimps in the hope of saving his own father, and by the third film it has become a full reckoning between two species fighting over what's left of the planet. Each film picks up where the last one left off, building one continuous story across three chapters without ever losing the emotional thread. No filler. No reset buttons. Just one tight, escalating arc from beginning to end.

## Why I Love These Movies

What I love about this trilogy is how quietly it does everything right. No single film announces itself as a masterpiece. Each one just comes in, does its job, and leaves you sitting there a little stunned by how much you cared. **Rise** is an origin story that builds every emotional beat without leaning on spectacle. **Dawn** is a morally complex war film dressed up as a blockbuster sequel that most blockbuster sequels would not have the nerve to attempt. **War** is a biblical epic that closes the whole thing out with the exact amount of weight it deserves.

Part of why Dawn and War feel so cohesive is that Matt Reeves took over for both, after Rupert Wyatt directed the first one. Reeves reportedly came in on a script that had been written before he signed on, one that pushed the apes further into the future and gave Caesar a smaller role, and asked to rebuild it from the ground up around Caesar's perspective instead. That decision is the reason this trilogy reads as one continuous character study instead of three separate movies wearing the same skin. Three films, two directors, zero drop-off in quality.

The thing that makes all three work is the same thing: **character first, spectacle second**. Every action sequence in this trilogy exists because of something a character is going through. The Golden Gate Bridge sequence in Rise works because you spent the whole film watching Caesar become the kind of leader capable of it. The dam standoff in Dawn works because you understand every person on both sides of it. The finale of War hits the way it does because you have been with Caesar across three films and you know exactly what it cost him to get there. None of it is decoration. All of it is story.

## The Characters

![The Planet of the Apes Trilogy is Underrated](/images/posts/the-planet-of-the-apes-trilogy-is-underrated/image-2.jpg)

**CAESAR:** The center of gravity for all three films and one of the best protagonists in modern blockbuster cinema. Andy Serkis builds him from a curious, wide-eyed chimp in Rise into a grieving, conflicted leader in War, and every step of that journey feels completely deserved. Serkis does all of this through motion capture, without a single frame of his actual face on screen. That's a remarkable feat of acting, and the Academy still owes him an apology for never giving performance capture the recognition it deserves, even with multiple Saturn Award wins backing him up.

**MAURICE:** Played by Karin Konoval, the gentle giant of the group and the conscience of the whole operation. The big orangutan who barely raises his voice but somehow always says the most important thing. Every great ensemble needs a Maurice.

**ROCKET:** Played by Terry Notary, who built his entire mo-cap career studying ape and primate movement, and you can feel that training in every scene. When we first meet him in Rise, he's the bully of the primate shelter. Aggressive, dominant, not someone you root for. By the time War rolls around he's one of the first to break rank and follow Caesar on a mission that could get them all killed, purely because he won't let his friend go alone. That arc, from antagonist to devoted companion, is one of the most satisfying character journeys in the whole trilogy.

**LUCA:** Luca doesn't get nearly as much screen time as the others, but his presence carries a quiet dignity the film never has to explain. The loyal gorilla who rides with Caesar on the most dangerous part of the whole journey, and in the moments where the film needs a visual of what loyalty actually looks like, Luca is it.

## The Villains

**KOBA:** One of the best movie villains of the 2010s and I will die on that hill. Toby Kebbell plays him as a bonobo who was tortured and experimented on by humans for most of his life. His hatred is fully earned by what he went through. The humans really did do a number on him, and watching him channel all of that pain into something destructive and calculating is disturbing. He doesn't see the peace Caesar is building as survival. He sees it as submission. The scariest part is that you don't agree with him, but you completely get it. Every scene he shares with Caesar in Dawn is quietly building toward his betrayal, and Kebbell plays the tension so well that you find yourself watching Koba even when he's not the focus of the scene.

**COLONEL McCULLOUGH:** Exactly the kind of villain War needed for its finale. His full name, J. Wesley McCullough, barely matters next to what Woody Harrelson does with him. He's not driven by cruelty or wounded pride but by cold, terrified logic, a belief that wiping out the apes is the only way to save what's left of humanity from the *Simian Flu*. What makes him work as the villain is the personal cost he inflicts before we even properly meet him: he kills Caesar's wife Cornelia and their eldest son Blue Eyes in a raid early in the film, which is exactly why Caesar's quest for revenge feels less like a plot device and more like grief with nowhere else to go. He's something different, a man who has convinced himself that his worst impulses are actually a form of love.

## Why It Works

Every action sequence exists because of something a character is going through. The visual effects, handled by WETA Digital across all three films, are some of the best ever committed to screen and hold up completely on rewatch today. But the effects were always in service of the performances underneath them. When Caesar is grieving, you see it in his face. When Koba performs friendliness while rage sits just underneath, you catch it in his eyes before anything else in the frame tips you off. The technology gave the actors a way to be fully present, and the result is that you never stop to think about how the apes were made. You just watch them like they're there.

The fact that the franchise was confident enough in this trilogy's foundation to keep building on it, with Kingdom of the Planet of the Apes picking up the story generations later in 2024, says everything about how much groundwork these three films actually laid. That's partly what this post is for.

---

The Planet of the Apes reboot trilogy is one of the best things blockbuster filmmaking produced in the 2010s, and it's not particularly close. Three films. One continuous story. Zero filler. Character-first storytelling dressed up in stunning visuals, with a lead performance that should have changed the conversation around motion capture entirely. If you haven't seen it, fix that immediately. If you have, you already know I'm right. Hall of Fame. No debate whatsoever.
