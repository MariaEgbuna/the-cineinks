The Planet of the Apes reboot trilogy does not get nearly enough credit, and I am tired of pretending otherwise.

These are not just good blockbusters, they are great. And if you have actually sat through all three, you already know I am not being dramatic.

Rise came out in 2011 on a $93 million budget and pulled $481.8 million worldwide. Nobody saw that coming. This was a franchise that had already been rebooted once in 2001 under Tim Burton and landed with a thud critically despite turning a profit. The idea of going back to the well again felt desperate rather than promising. It was more than fine. It was the start of one of the most quietly consistent trilogies in modern blockbuster filmmaking. Dawn followed in 2014 and grossed over $710 million worldwide on a budget that ballooned past $170 million. War closed it out in 2017, earning $490 million globally off a roughly $150 million budget. Three films. $1.5 billion combined. Critical scores of 81%, 91%, and 94% on Rotten Tomatoes respectively. A franchise that got better with every single entry. Hall of Fame. No debate.

---

### The Plot (In a Nutshell)

A genetically enhanced chimpanzee named Caesar grows up caught between the human world that raised him and the ape world he belongs to, eventually leading a rebellion that changes the course of both species forever. It starts small, a San Francisco scientist testing an Alzheimer's drug on chimps in the hope of saving his own father, and by the third film it has become a full reckoning between two species fighting over what's left of the planet. Each film picks up where the last one left off, building one continuous story across three chapters without ever losing the emotional thread. No filler. No reset buttons. Just one tight, escalating arc from beginning to end.

### My Take

What gets me about this trilogy is how quietly it does everything right. No single film announces itself as a masterpiece. Each one just comes in, does its job with real craft and real feeling, and leaves you sitting there a little stunned by how much you cared. Rise is an origin story that builds every emotional beat without leaning on spectacle. Dawn is a morally complex war film dressed up as a blockbuster sequel that most blockbuster sequels would not have the nerve to attempt. War is a biblical epic that closes the whole thing out with the exact amount of weight it deserves.

Part of why Dawn and War feel so cohesive is that Matt Reeves took over for both, after Rupert Wyatt directed the first one. Reeves reportedly came in on a script that had been written before he signed on, one that pushed the apes further into the future and gave Caesar a smaller role, and asked to rebuild it from the ground up around Caesar's perspective instead. That decision is the reason this trilogy reads as one continuous character study instead of three separate movies wearing the same skin. Three films, two directors, zero drop-off in quality.

The thing that makes all three work is the same thing: character first, spectacle second. Every action sequence in this trilogy exists because of something a character is going through. The Golden Gate Bridge sequence in Rise works because you spent the whole film watching Caesar become the kind of leader capable of it. The dam standoff in Dawn works because you understand every person on both sides of it. The finale of War hits the way it does because you have been with Caesar across three films and you know exactly what it cost him to get there. None of it is decoration. All of it is story.

### The Characters

![The Planet of the Apes Trilogy is Underrated](/images/posts/the-planet-of-the-apes-trilogy-is-underrated/image-2.jpg)

**CAESAR:** The center of gravity for all three films and one of the great protagonists in modern blockbuster cinema. Andy Serkis builds him from a curious, wide-eyed chimp in Rise into a grieving, conflicted leader in War, and every step of that journey feels completely deserved. The moment in War where Caesar starts recognizing Koba's anger in himself is the best thing the trilogy does, a character terrified of becoming the thing he spent three films fighting against. Serkis does all of this through motion capture, without a single frame of his actual face on screen. That's a remarkable feat of acting, and the Academy still owes him an apology for never giving performance capture the recognition it deserves, even with multiple Saturn Award wins backing him up.

**MAURICE:** Played by Karin Konoval, the gentle giant of the group and the conscience of the whole operation. The big orangutan who barely raises his voice but somehow always says the most important thing. There's a moment in War where he insists on taking in a mute human girl named Nova that nobody else wants to deal with, and it tells you everything about who Maurice is without a single speech. He just does it. Quiet, observant, and steady in a way that makes him the emotional anchor of every scene he's in. Every great ensemble needs a Maurice.

**ROCKET:** Played by Terry Notary, who built his entire mo-cap career studying ape and primate movement, and you can feel that training in every scene. When we first meet him in Rise, he's the bully of the primate shelter. Aggressive, dominant, not someone you root for. By the time War rolls around he's one of the first to break rank and follow Caesar on a mission that could get them all killed, purely because he won't let his friend go alone. That arc, from antagonist to devoted companion, is one of the most quietly satisfying character journeys in the whole trilogy. No big speeches. No dramatic turning point moment. Just a character who changed slowly and believably over three films. That kind of restraint is hard to execute and easy to take for granted.

**LUCA:** Luca doesn't get nearly as much screen time as the others, but his presence carries a quiet dignity the film never has to explain. The loyal gorilla who rides with Caesar on the most dangerous part of the whole journey, and in the moments where the film needs a visual of what loyalty actually looks like, Luca is it.

### Honorable Mentions

**KOBA:** One of the best movie villains of the 2010s and I will die on that hill. Toby Kebbell plays him as a bonobo who was tortured and experimented on by humans for most of his life, and his hatred is not cartoonish or randomly generated. It is fully earned by what he went through. The humans really did do a number on him, and watching him channel all of that pain into something destructive and calculating is disturbing in the best way. He doesn't see the peace Caesar is building as survival. He sees it as submission. The scariest part is that you don't agree with him, but you completely get it. Every scene he shares with Caesar in Dawn is quietly building toward his betrayal, and Kebbell plays the tension so well that you find yourself watching Koba even when he's not the focus of the scene.

**COLONEL McCULLOUGH:** Exactly the kind of villain War needed for its finale. His full name, J. Wesley McCullough, barely matters next to what Woody Harrelson does with him. He's not driven by cruelty or wounded pride but by cold, terrified logic, a belief that wiping out the apes is the only way to save what's left of humanity from the Simian Flu. What makes him hit harder than a standard movie villain is the personal cost he inflicts before we even properly meet him: he kills Caesar's wife Cornelia and their eldest son Blue Eyes in a raid early in the film, which is exactly why Caesar's quest for revenge feels less like a plot device and more like grief with nowhere else to go. Harrelson plays McCullough's conviction so carefully that there are moments where the argument almost lands before you catch yourself. He's not Koba. He's something different and in some ways more unsettling, a man who has convinced himself that his worst impulses are actually a form of love.

**BAD APE:** Steve Zahn's Bad Ape is the comic relief War desperately needed and used at exactly the right moments. A hermit ape who escaped a Portland zoo enclosure and survived the Simian Flu years in total isolation, he has no idea how to be around other apes anymore, and that disconnect is where most of the comedy comes from. He brings warmth and real laughs to a film that is otherwise carrying enormous emotional weight.

**CHARLES RODMAN:** John Lithgow's performance as Will's father, whose Alzheimer's is the original reason the ALZ-112 drug got developed in the first place, gives Rise its most vulnerable and most human moments. The disease taking him piece by piece while Caesar watches is the quiet emotional underpinning of everything that follows. A small role that does enormous work.

### Why It Works

This trilogy works because it never once forgets that spectacle without character is just noise. Every action sequence exists because of something a character is going through. The visual effects, handled by WETA Digital across all three films, are some of the best ever committed to screen and hold up completely on rewatch today. But the effects were always in service of the performances underneath them. When Caesar is grieving, you see it in his face in the specific, quiet way a person looks when they're trying to hold it together and failing. When Koba performs friendliness while rage sits just underneath, you catch it in his eyes before anything else in the frame tips you off. The technology gave the actors a way to be fully present, and the result is that you never stop to think about how the apes were made. You just watch them like they're there.

The one honest criticism is that War underperformed commercially relative to its scale, earning $490 million on a budget reported anywhere from $150 to $190 million, which turns into a thin profit once marketing costs get factored in. The trilogy never got the mainstream cultural recognition it deserved during its original run, which is baffling given the quality. Rise got 81% on Rotten Tomatoes. Dawn got 91%. War got 94%. A franchise that improved with every entry, treated its audience as intelligent adults, and stuck its landing. It just did it quietly, and quiet excellence gets overlooked more often than it should. The fact that the franchise was confident enough in this trilogy's foundation to keep building on it, with Kingdom of the Planet of the Apes picking up the story generations later in 2024, says everything about how much groundwork these three films actually laid. That's partly what this post is for.

---

### Final Verdict

The Planet of the Apes reboot trilogy is one of the best things blockbuster filmmaking produced in the 2010s, and it's not particularly close. Three films. One continuous story. Zero filler. Character-first storytelling dressed up in stunning visuals, with a lead performance that should have changed the conversation around motion capture entirely. If you haven't seen it, fix that immediately. If you have, you already know I'm right. Hall of Fame. No debate whatsoever.
