Call It Love, also released under the title Say It's Love in some regions, is a 2023 South Korean romantic melodrama that premiered on Disney+ on February 22 and wrapped up on April 12 that same year. It ran for sixteen hour-long episodes, directed by Lee Kwang-young and written by Kim Ga-eun. The cast is led by Lee Sung-kyung as Shim Woo-joo and Kim Young-kwang as Han Dong-jin, with Sung Joon playing Yoon Joon and Kim Ye-won as Woo-joo's older sister. It's a Studio Dragon and Drama House production, and it landed decently well with critics and audiences when it aired, praised mostly for its performances and its quieter, more grounded take on the revenge romance formula.

A quick heads up before I go further: **this review has minor spoilers.** Nothing that ruins the ending, but I do talk about plot direction and pacing in enough detail that you might want to bookmark this and come back after you've started the show, if you're the type who likes going in completely blind and unspoiled.

---

## The setup

Woo-joo's life falls apart when her father dies. He had a mistress on the side, one who then has the audacity to kick Woo-joo and her family out of their own home. Understandably furious, Woo-joo decides she wants revenge, and she zeroes in on Han Dong-jin, the mistress's son, as her target. The problem is that Dong-jin turns out to be nothing like the smug, spoiled rich kid she expected. He's gentle, quietly lonely, and dealing with his own baggage, and the longer Woo-joo spends around him plotting her payback, the more she starts falling for him instead.

It's a familiar setup if you've watched any amount of Korean melodrama. Revenge plot collides with a slow-burn romance, misunderstandings pile up, everyone's too polite to just say what they're feeling until episode eleven. Call It Love leans hard into the "gentle soul healing a wounded soul" dynamic rather than going for spectacle, which is exactly where my feelings about this show start getting complicated.

## Kim Young-kwang in a role I didn't know he had in him

![Kim Young-kwang](/images/posts/kyk-1-.jpg)

I've seen Kim Young-kwang in a handful of things before this, and every single time, he's been cast as some flavor of villain. He played a terrifying psychopathic killer in **Somebody** back in 2022, and more recently he showed up as a menacing crime figure in **Trigger**. Even outside the shows I've personally watched, his reputation online leans heavily toward "the guy who plays unsettling bad guys really, really well". So watching him play Dong-jin, a soft-spoken, emotionally guarded but fundamentally kind man, was a lovely surprise.

And he's really good at it. There's a stillness to how he plays Dong-jin, a quiet sadness sits behind his eyes even in scenes where he's supposed to be fine. It's a completely different register from anything I'd seen him do, and it makes total sense that this role is the one a lot of people point to as his career highlight, the performance that convinced a rom-com audience he could actually act outside of being terrifying. 

Honestly, he's the entire reason I decided to watch this show in the first place, and even with all my complaints about the rest of it, I don't regret that decision. His performance alone justified the time.

## Where the story starts dragging its feet

![Screenshot from CIL](/images/posts/cil.jpg)

Now for the part where I have some real issues. The story, for lack of a better way to put it, feels stretched way past the point where it actually had anything left to say. Maybe that's on me for signing up for a melodrama and being surprised it moved slowly, that's kind of the genre's whole personality. But even accounting for that, sixteen episodes is a lot of runtime for a story this thin.

I held out for the actual romance to become official, properly official, until around episode twelve. And the second I got what I'd been waiting for, I immediately started losing interest, because it became obvious the show was deliberately killing time rather than building toward anything new. You can feel the difference between a slow burn that's actually developing character and a slow burn that's just stalling because the season needs to hit a certain episode count. Call It Love starts in the first category and quietly slides into the second somewhere around the midpoint.

This show should have been twelve episodes, maybe thirteen, and called it a day. The story beats are fairly predictable once you understand the genre conventions it's working within, so there was no real narrative need to stretch it out this far. A tighter cut of this same show, trimming the repetitive miscommunication scenes and the padding in the back half, would have hit so much harder. Melodramas live and die by how well they use their runtime, and this one spends a good chunk of its final third circling ideas it already resolved earlier, rather than pushing the relationship or the family conflict into new territory.

## The revenge plot that barely exists

This is probably my biggest gripe, and it's the one that genuinely confused me the most. The show opens with Woo-joo swearing revenge against the family that wronged hers, and I went in expecting something with real teeth, telenovela-level scheming, sabotage, someone getting publicly humiliated at a company gala, that kind of thing. What I actually got was Woo-joo getting a job at Dong-jin's company and then, functionally, doing nothing with that position. The revenge angle gets set up with real weight in the opening episodes and then just quietly deflates as the show shifts its full attention to the romance.

I understand the thematic point being made here, that empathy and understanding can dissolve the need for revenge once you actually get to know the person you wanted to hurt. That's a nice idea for a melodrama to sit with. But structurally, if you're going to open your entire show on a revenge premise, you need to *actually do something with it* before quietly abandoning it in favor of longing looks and unspoken feelings. As it stands, the revenge plot feels less like a real storyline and more like a hook to get you invested before the show pivots entirely into straightforward romance. I don't understand why so many people rate this drama as highly as they do, when one of its two core premises barely gets developed at all.

## Episodes fourteen and fifteen nearly broke me

I want to specifically call out episodes fourteen and fifteen, because even accounting for how slow melodramas tend to be in general, these two episodes tested my patience in a way the rest of the show hadn't. By this point, the major emotional beats had already landed, and there just wasn't much left except waiting for the finale to arrive. These episodes felt like the show treading water, recycling emotional beats it had already covered better earlier in the run. If you make it this far, be prepared to push through a genuinely sluggish stretch before the payoff.

## The supporting cast carries more weight than you'd expect

![Call it Love Cast](/images/posts/cil-cast.jpg)

It's not just Kim Young-kwang and Lee Sung-kyung doing the heavy lifting here. Sung Joon plays Yoon Joon, a friend caught in his own tangle of unspoken feelings, and while his arc mostly exists to add a layer of will-they-won't-they tension on the sidelines, he brings a warmth to the role that keeps those scenes from feeling like pure filler. Kim Ye-won, playing Woo-joo's older sister, gets pulled into a parallel storyline involving the decades-old rivalry between their mother and the woman who broke up their parents' marriage in the first place. That older generation's drama runs quietly underneath the main romance the whole time, and there were stretches where I found myself more curious about how that older feud would resolve than I was about the central couple, especially once the main romance started running out of new ground to cover.

## A genre known for testing patience

Melodrama as a genre asks a lot of its audience by design. It trades big, plot-driven momentum for lingering close-ups, quiet confessions, and scenes that let an emotional moment sit far longer than a Western drama typically would. When it works, that slowness becomes the whole appeal, you're not watching for what happens next, you're watching for how a character's face changes when they finally admit something to themselves. Call It Love clearly wants to live in that space, and for its first ten or so episodes, it mostly earns the slowness. Woo-joo's guardedness melting around Dong-jin, and his own loneliness cracking open around her, was so beautiful to watch.

Where the show loses me is that it never seems to recognize the exact moment its slow-burn has finished burning. A good melodrama knows when to let quiet scenes breathe and when to actually move the story forward. Call It Love mistakes stillness for depth in its back half, repeating variations the leads already worked through, just dressed up in a new setting or a new minor conflict.

## Critical reception versus how I actually felt

For what it's worth, Call It Love was fairly well received when it aired, with plenty of praise going toward its performances and its more grounded, less theatrical approach to the genre compared to flashier revenge dramas. A lot of viewers specifically praised how it prioritized quiet character work over plot mechanics, treating the show less as a plot-driven revenge story and more as a character study wrapped in a romance. I understand that appeal on paper, and I even felt it myself for a good chunk of the run.

So long, unnecessarily long, that's really the short version of my entire experience with Call It Love. The performances are the reason to watch this, particularly Kim Young-kwang doing something completely outside his usual range and doing it well, and Lee Sung-kyung holding her own as a woman whose anger keeps getting complicated by her feelings. The romance itself, once it actually gets going, has real chemistry behind it. But the pacing works against almost everything the show does well, stretching a story that could have wrapped up cleanly in twelve episodes into sixteen, and largely abandoning its own revenge premise somewhere along the way.

**My rating: 6.8/10**, and that's almost entirely carried by the romance and the acting. If either of those had been weaker, this would have been a much rougher watch given how much patience the show demands. I'd still recommend to someone specifically chasing a quiet, character-driven melodrama, or anyone curious about Kim Young-kwang outside of his usual villain roles, but go in with a trimmed set of expectations for the revenge angle and a decent amount of patience banked for the final stretch.
