The actual finale episode was not that bad. I kind of liked it. My real issue is with the season as a whole.

The story, the power scaling, the budget, the character decisions, all of it. When you compare where this show started with where it ended, the distance between the two is painful.

*This post contains spoilers for The Boys on Amazon Prime.*

---

## What this show was at its best

The Boys is an Amazon Prime Video series created by Eric Kripke, who also created Supernatural, based on the comic by Garth Ennis and Darick Robertson. It premiered on July 26, 2019, and became the most watched series Prime Video had ever released at the time. Five seasons, forty episodes, and a sharp premise: what if the most powerful beings on earth were brand assets managed by a corporation, and the only people trying to stop them were broken civilians with no powers and nothing left to lose?

Seasons 1 and 2 had a clear architecture. Hughie loses his girlfriend Robin in Episode 1 when A-Train runs through her at full speed while fleeing a crime scene. He doesn't even stop. That moment is the show's thesis in four seconds: to a supe, a human life isn't even an obstacle. Vought International was the ultimate villain, a media empire that turns PR into impunity and wraps atrocities in branding. The scariest thing about power wasn't the individual who wielded it, but the system that made them untouchable.

Homelander worked because that system contained him. He was constrained by Vought's PR concerns and by his need to perform heroism to keep his power, and that tension was the engine of everything. Antony Starr played him like a loaded gun that hadn't decided whether to go off.

## Who is this show even about?

At some point, the show forgot that Hughie and Annie were its main characters and Vought was its villain. They're the emotional center, two people being crushed from opposite sides, and when the writers lose track of them, the structure loosens. Around Season 3, it became the Butcher and Homelander show, and everyone else got shortchanged. Starlight most of all. By the final season she felt stagnant, like the writers had no idea what to do with her and decided the answer was fewer scenes and more standing in the background looking concerned. She spent four seasons fighting to carve out an identity inside a machine built to erase it, and she deserved a final season that matched that.

Too many characters also overstayed their welcome because the writers were too cautious to kill anyone who mattered until the very end. Frenchie is the clearest example. His storylines had been running on diminishing returns since Season 3, and the show kept manufacturing new arcs for him instead of letting him go.

Then there's Victoria Neuman, a head-popping congresswoman using Vought's own methods against everyone around her. Seasons of setup, and then at the end of Season 4 Butcher uses his tentacle powers to tear her apart in a scene that should have been cathartic and just felt abrupt. A major political threat with real potential left, killed to clear the board for a final season that set up spinoffs instead of paying off her arc. What a waste. And those tentacles were an eyesore from start to finish. The visual was wrong, the power set contradicted the show's own line between supes and humans, and it felt like a choice made to let Butcher kill Neuman instead of something that came out of the story.

## The budget and the pacing

The budget in Season 5 went off a cliff, and I refuse to be talked out of this. Watch Season 3 Episode 6, where Soldier Boy, Butcher, and Hughie take on Homelander with actual combined force. The choreography, the stakes, the character beats inside the action: that's what a big set piece looks like when the writing and production work together. The final season should have reached for that. Instead, by multiple accounts, the finale delivered the deaths and the gore but not the catharsis. The action felt under-resourced and the staging felt constrained, like it was filmed under limitations the show couldn't overcome. This was the last eight episodes of a show that became a cultural phenomenon, and the scale didn't match the moment.

The pacing collapsed the same way. Characters switching sides, major deaths, and revelations that needed room were all piled on top of each other in the final episodes. Butcher killing Homelander with a crowbar to the skull is exactly the ending that story deserved, but the sequence around it needed to breathe.

## The power scaling problem

This has been unraveling since Season 3. Across the first two seasons, Homelander has hearing so acute he can pick up a heartbeat from across a building. He clocks A-Train muttering under his breath and Frenchie tapping a finger from another room. Add super strength, flight, laser vision, and near-total invulnerability, and he's nearly impossible to hide from. That's why he's so terrifying.

Then, whenever the plot needs characters to have a private conversation or run a plan, those abilities just stop. His powers get switched on and off based on what the scene needs instead of the rules the show set. That's lazy writing, and it disrespects an audience that paid attention. If you build a villain who's nearly omniscient, you have to write around that consistently.

### The Gen V crossover

Marie Moreau was built up across a whole spinoff as a powerhouse who could shift the balance between supes and humans. The crossover was supposed to be the payoff. She crossed over into Season 5, brought a notebook, and escorted some people to Canada. That's it. If you can skip an entire spinoff, watch its characters show up on the main show, and still not miss anything that matters to the plot, the spinoff shouldn't have been positioned as essential viewing. It wasted the audience's goodwill and an interesting character.

### What actually worked

To be fair, a couple of things worked.

**A-Train** had the best individual story of the final season. He started as the supe who killed Hughie's girlfriend without breaking stride, literally the inciting incident of the series. His five-season arc is a man who chose power over conscience, then over humanity, and finally, too late to save himself, chose to become something worth remembering. He spent his last episode helping The Boys escape the Freedom Camps, and Homelander killed him for it. He'd finally become a hero again, and it cost him everything.

**Firecracker** had one of the most unsettling arcs of the season, and it went exactly where the show needed. She entered as the ultimate true believer, weaponizing her own trauma to build a platform for Homelander, and realized too late that the god she worshiped saw her as a disposable prop. She spent her final moments trying to stay relevant while the walls closed in, tied to a man who didn't care if she lived or died.

---

### So, how was the ending?

The finale episode was okay. Butcher killing Homelander with a crowbar while saying "this is for Becca" is the right ending to that story. The epilogue has some real sentiment: Hughie and Annie expecting a child named Robin, Mother's Milk becoming Ryan's legal guardian, Kimiko moving to Marseille to honor Frenchie's memory. The show remembering who these people are when the violence stops is where it does its best character work.

But the season as a whole isn't close to what this show could do at its peak. The writing prioritized setting up future franchise content over concluding the story it had been telling. It ran out of road for some characters two seasons too late and out of ideas for others two seasons too early.

Seasons 1 and 2 are still some of the smartest, nastiest, most unsettling television of the 2020s, and the finale doesn't erase that. But a show this good deserved a final season that matched what it was capable of. It didn't get one.

**Sad, but it is what it is.**
