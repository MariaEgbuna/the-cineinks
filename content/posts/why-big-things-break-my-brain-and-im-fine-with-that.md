![Why Big Things Break My Brain (And I'm Fine With That)](/images/posts/why-big-things-break-my-brain-and-im-fine-with-that/image-1.jpg)

Big, loud, and world-shattering spectacle beats deep, boring, high-brow cinema every single day of the week. If there is a massive creature on screen capable of casually stepping on a skyscraper, I am unconditionally seated in the front row. 

I don't need to see a trailer, or check the Rotten Tomatoes score, or listen to a random film bro on the internet saying "the CGI looks a bit unpolished" in the third act. Do you know why? It's because I simply do not care. Consider this essay my formal attempt to explain exactly why that is — and why I have absolutely zero plans to change. 

This isn't a review of any singular movie; it’s a fierce, unyielding defense of an entire way of experiencing art. Pure cinematic spectacle is constantly treated like a guilty pleasure — something you are expected to outgrow or apologize for once you develop a supposedly "sophisticated" taste.

## The Obsession Has an Origin Story

This fixation has been the literal foundation of my personality since childhood. The exact second my brain saw something massive, impossible, and world-ending on a screen, it immediately decided: *Yes. More of this, please.* 

It all started with Peter Jackson’s 2005 *King Kong* remake. The film famously cost a staggering $207 million, ran for over three glorious hours, and stood as the most expensive movie ever made at the time. Universal executives signed off on every single budget overrun because they knew what they had. 

I remember sitting in front of the television completely paralyzed, eyes locked onto the glass. Seeing a digital creature of that monumental size — imbued with that much presence, weight, and emotional gravity — permanently rewired my brain chemistry. Kong wasn't just big; he was built entirely different. The sheer scale and the raw emotion hit me at the exact same millisecond, and I have never fully recovered. From that definitive moment onward, the only qualification a film needed to win my loyalty was a simple question: *Does something completely enormous exist in this universe?* That was the entire bar, and it has never once failed me.

## The MonsterVerse: A Defense of Not Caring About the Script

![Why Big Things Break My Brain (And I'm Fine With That)](/images/posts/why-big-things-break-my-brain-and-im-fine-with-that/image-2.jpg)

I will be the absolute first to admit that Legendary's MonsterVerse films are not exactly targeted for Academy Awards consideration. The human characters in these movies are mostly just organic placeholders who exist solely to point frantically at the sky and deliver breathless exposition like *"We have to stop it!"* or *"Nothing could have prepared us for this!"* meanwhile, the entire audience is collectively thinking: *Please shut up and put the camera back on the radioactive lizard.*

Is the screenwriting occasionally held together by nothing but hope, vibes, and good intentions? Yes, absolutely. 

Do I care? Not even a little bit. 

Here is the thing about MonsterVerse criticism that never quite lands for me: when critics complain that "the human characters are weak," they are describing a feature as if it's a bug. The human characters are weak because they are fundamentally not the point of the exercise. They are merely the plain wooden frame around a magnificent painting. You don't look at a masterpiece in a museum and complain about the border. The frame exists purely so the painting has a physical place to live. 

When Godzilla violently rises out of the ocean, the theater bass drops, and an entity of that scale moves with terrifying, impossible speed — that is the entire movie. The plot is just the loading screen; **the monster is the destination.** I have never once felt cheated by these films because I know exactly what I am paying for. 

## Pacific Rim: A Masterpiece Full of Characters I Cannot Name

![Why Big Things Break My Brain (And I'm Fine With That)](/images/posts/why-big-things-break-my-brain-and-im-fine-with-that/image-3.jpg)

If you held a gun to my head right now, I could not tell you the legal name of a single human character in Guillermo del Toro's *Pacific Rim* (2013). There was a generic main guy, he had some emotional baggage about a past trauma, and there was a stoic mentor figure who gave an inspirational speech. There was definitely a tragic backstory involving loss that I was structurally supposed to be weeping over. 

What I can tell you, with crystalline, high-definition clarity, is exactly what it felt like to watch a Jaeger step out of the dark ocean for the first time. The Jaegers don't move like slick, weightless Transformers; they move slowly, deliberately, with millions of tons of grinding, hydraulic weight behind every single stride. A machine the size of an entire block of apartments, wading through ocean water that barely reaches its metal knees, built for the singular, glorious purpose of walking up to an alien monster and punching it directly in the jaw. The human characters could have literally been replaced by cardboard cutouts reading "Human One" and "Human Two" and I still would have given the movie a perfect score based entirely on the mech footage. Zero regrets. Zero apologies.

## Jurassic Park: A Franchise That Lost the Plot (And I’m Still Seated)

![Why Big Things Break My Brain (And I'm Fine With That)](/images/posts/why-big-things-break-my-brain-and-im-fine-with-that/image-4.jpg)

The original 1993 *Jurassic Park* is a masterpiece. Steven Spielberg captured lightning in a bottle, creating a cinematic experience that has never been fully replicated despite thirty years of technological advancements. That iconic sequence where the T-Rex steps out into the pouring rain, the water trembling in the plastic cup, and lets out that terrifying, echoing roar is one of cinema's all-time greatest hold-your-breath moments.

The sequels, conversely, are a wild ride. The *Jurassic World* trilogy kept inventing increasingly absurd, corporate excuses to send people back to an island that has systematically murdered every single human who has ever stepped foot on it. The screenwriting progressively deteriorated, and by 2022's *Dominion*, the franchise had run completely out of creative steam, relying on legacy nostalgia and a bizarre prehistoric locust subplot that absolutely nobody asked for. 

And yet, I watched every single one. Because a CGI dinosaur walking through a frame is an automatic, unconditional victory for me. A Tyrannosaurus Rex on screen produces an instinctual, biological response in my chest that entirely bypasses the analytical part of my brain that cares about character arcs or three-act structures. You could hand me two hours of the worst dialogue ever committed to paper, but if an apex predator of that scale exists within the frame, I will walk out of that theater thoroughly satisfied.

## Attack on Titan: Where Scale Becomes Absolute Art

![Why Big Things Break My Brain (And I'm Fine With That)](/images/posts/why-big-things-break-my-brain-and-im-fine-with-that/image-5.jpg)

This is the point where I have to pause my "dumb blockbuster" defense and admit that something profoundly deeper is actually happening under the surface. *Attack on Titan* figured out a narrative secret that Hollywood spends hundreds of millions of dollars trying to manufacture but almost never achieves: that scale, when weaponized correctly, isn't just visually cool — it is psychologically destabilizing in the best possible way. 

You know the exact scene from the very first episode. The Colossal Titan slowly peers over Wall Maria. The wall itself is fifty meters tall — a staggering, monumental structure that represents the absolute boundary of human survival, a fortress humanity spent a century building just to feel safe. And the Colossal Titan is simply taller. It peers over the edge of that historic stronghold the exact same way a normal person would casually look over a neighborhood garden fence. 

In that single frame, my brain went entirely quiet. I was filled with absolute, pristine awe — the exact same pristine feeling I have been chasing since I was a little kid sitting frozen in front of a television set. 

## The Psychology of the Mega-Monster

This psychological reaction actually has a formal name, and it is vastly older than the invention of motion pictures. In 1757, the philosopher Edmund Burke published a treatise exploring why certain extreme human experiences produce a feeling that isn't quite standard fear or standard pleasure, but hangs beautifully right between both. 

He called this concept **The Sublime**. 

Burke’s core argument was that the Sublime occurs when our senses are confronted with something so infinitely vast, powerful, or hazardous that it completely overwhelms our rational ability to process it. Faced with something of that magnitude, your brain simply hits its structural processing limit and goes completely silent in reverence. Burke was thinking about scaling massive mountain peaks or watching catastrophic ocean thunderstorms from a cliffside; I just happen to experience that exact same profound, existential hush while watching a fictional, radioactive lizard level a metropolitan city.

Humanity invented fiction to trigger this psychological response on demand, built massive IMAX theaters specifically to deliver it at maximum volume, and I will always show up for it.
